# Partner outreach

A good partner reaches the same people at a different moment, and gains something from working together. Open each pitch with something specific from their work, offer something their readers would want, and ask for one small yes or no. You draft and qualify. The user decides who gets contacted, and sends the messages.

## Ask the user first

Pitches fail on missing specifics, so get the offer to partners (item 2) before drafting anything. Mark other gaps `[CONFIRM: ...]`.

1. What they sell and who buys it. A buyer brief from buyer-research helps. Without one, use the user's description of the buyer and say so.
2. What they can offer a partner: expertise to write or speak about, audience size and engagement per channel, original data, a product to bundle, codes for free access, a referral or affiliate rate, placement in their own product or emails, and any cash budget for sponsorships.
3. What a new customer is worth to them, so the offer to partners can be checked.
4. What they want: paying customers, audience growth, links and mentions, credibility.
5. Partners they already have, and any to avoid (competitors, categories that clash with their brand).
6. Rules to follow: disclosure of paid deals, health or financial claims, industry codes.
7. How many conversations they can handle at once, and the sender's name, role and website.

## Procedure

1. Map the overlap. Write down what the customer does right before, during and after buying, using the prompts in [references/qualify.md](references/qualify.md). For each moment, list who else serves them without competing. A home organizer's customers are often moving, renovating, downsizing or having a baby. So the partners are agents, movers, interior designers, storage companies and parenting newsletters.
2. Find named prospects for each partner type. Aim for 20 to 40 in a first round. Search the customer's problems in the user's market. Look in podcast directories and newsletter platforms, Facebook and Reddit communities, YouTube and Instagram, and local business groups. Ask customers what else they buy, read or follow. Sites that link to competitors are fine as a source, even though competitors themselves aren't partners. Audience research tools can suggest names. Check each one against the partner's own recent content.
3. Screen quickly: active in the last 60 days, and plausibly reaching the buyer. Drop the rest. Keep anyone dormant who fits unusually well on a "revisit later" list.
4. Score the survivors with the scorecard in [references/qualify.md](references/qualify.md). Write one sentence on why each is relevant, from something specific you saw. If you can't write it, drop them. If fewer qualify than planned, deliver the ones that do, plus a short plan for the partner types still to research.
5. Choose one contribution per partner, to match how they work. Do they take guest posts? Have a sponsor page or an affiliate program? Interview guests? Run events? Name the piece, its length, where it runs and what you bring, for example: "A 1,200-word guide on clearing a house before listing photos, for your seller newsletter, with our before-and-after photos."
6. Check the deal works for both sides. Work out what the partner would roughly earn or gain, for example 30 referrals × the affiliate rate. A creator who'd earn $40 a year from a deal won't answer. Check the partner's existing deals against the user's avoid list, and against their other prospects.
7. Draft each pitch (structure below), and one follow-up for five to seven working days later. The follow-up adds something new: a draft outline, a proposed date, a relevant result.
8. Plan the waves. Order the prospects by score, and split them into waves the user can handle (the first wave is often five). Start the next wave as replies come in.
9. Set up tracking. Give each partner its own UTM link and its own code. For apps, use the app store's campaign links or the user's attribution tool. Count a customer as someone who paid, not someone who redeemed a code.
10. Write a one-page outline of what happens after a yes. It covers what each side does and by when, how content may be used, who approves claims, payment terms, and a small pilot before anything bigger.

## The pitch

- Subject line: specific and under about 50 characters, for example "Guest guide for your seller newsletter?".
- First line: one specific, true reference to their work that shows why you picked them. If you can't check the detail, leave a `[CONFIRM: what they posted]` placeholder for the user. Never make one up.
- Next two lines: the proposal, and what their audience gets.
- One line of credibility, chosen for this recipient and kept under about ten words. A physiotherapist cares about the method more than the download count.
- The ask: small and easy to answer yes or no. "Would you like me to send an outline?"
- Keep the body under 120 words, not counting the subject and signature. Direct messages (Instagram, LinkedIn, community inboxes) should be under about 60 words. Send the detail once they reply. Sponsor and partner forms get the same content in the fields they ask for.
- Sign-off: full name, role, business and link.

## Journalist requests

Answer only where the user has relevant expertise and can meet the deadline. Lead with the answer to the question asked, in one or two sentences, then one line of credentials, then a fact, figure or example the journalist can check. Under 150 words, sent well before the deadline.

## Decision rules

- Send fewer pitches and choose them carefully. In Backlinko's 2019 study of 12 million outreach emails, 8.5% got a reply, so expect most pitches to go unanswered.
- One follow-up. In the same study, one follow-up was associated with about 66% more replies. After that, stop. Never follow up after a no or an opt-out.
- Pick a 3,000-reader newsletter in the buyer's niche over a 100,000-reader general one.
- Data in a pitch has to be defined before it's used: what was counted, over what period, and how many people. "Week 3 is when most beginners quit (48,000 users, 2025)" works only if the user can say how quitting was measured.
- Don't arrange link swaps whose main purpose is ranking. A link between partners is fine when it helps both sets of readers. Paid placements and affiliate links get `rel="sponsored"`, and paid relationships are disclosed wherever the content runs.
- Health, money and legal topics: anything a partner says about the product has to stay within what the user can prove and what advertising rules allow. List claims for the user to approve.
- Don't fake familiarity, and don't claim results, clients or press the user doesn't have.
- Cold email: say who you are, include a postal address and an opt-out, and honor opt-outs. In the UK, email companies, not sole traders or partnerships, unless they've agreed. In the EU the rules vary by country, and some (Germany, for example) need consent even for business addresses. Flag it for the user.

## Output

1. The partner map: customer moments, partner types, and why each fits.
2. The prospect table:

| Partner | Type | Why relevant | Fit 0-3 | Engagement 0-3 | Standards 0-3 | Total /9 | Active | Contribution | Contact route | Wave |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

3. Dropped and "revisit later" prospects, with reasons. Partner types still to research.
4. One pitch and one follow-up per prospect, labeled.
5. The after-yes outline.
6. The tracking sheet: partner, wave, sent date, follow-up date, reply, outcome, link or code, visits, paying customers, notes.
7. CONFIRM items.

## Example

A home organizing business in one city, with 600 newsletter subscribers, a large before-and-after photo archive and 90 five-star reviews. It wants referrals.

| Partner | Type | Why relevant | Fit | Eng. | Std. | Total | Active | Contribution | Contact route | Wave |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rivera Home Team | Real estate agents | Their listing photos show cluttered rooms, and their monthly seller email has a "prep tips" section | 3 | 2 | 3 | 8 | Posted last week | A prep guide for their seller email, plus 15% off for their clients | Office email on their site | 1 |

```text
Subject: Prep guide for your seller emails?

Hi Ana,

Your March seller email said cluttered closets were the thing you most often ask sellers to fix before photos. We clear homes for listing photos every week here in Eastport.

I'd like to write a one-page "48 hours before the photographer" guide for your sellers, with our own before-and-after photos, and offer your clients 15% off a pre-listing session.

We've organized about 200 local homes since 2022.

Want me to send a draft you can edit?

Jordan Lee, owner
Clear Space Organizing, clearspace.example
```

Follow-up, day 6: "Here's the draft guide as a shared doc. It's yours to edit or bin. No reply needed if the timing's wrong."

## Final check

- [ ] Every prospect has a specific reason based on their own work. Unverified details are marked CONFIRM.
- [ ] Scores have totals. Active is a pass or fail gate, and dropped prospects have reasons.
- [ ] Each pitch has one concrete contribution and one small ask, within the length limit for its channel.
- [ ] The credential line suits the recipient. Any data quoted is defined.
- [ ] The deal is worth answering for the partner, and doesn't clash with the avoid list.
- [ ] No faked familiarity, results, clients or press. Paid deals are flagged for disclosure.
- [ ] Each follow-up adds something new, and only one is planned.
- [ ] Waves fit the user's capacity. Each partner has its own link or code, and customers are counted as paying.
- [ ] Nothing has been sent.
