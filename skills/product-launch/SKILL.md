# Product launch

A small launch is two to four weeks of posts and emails aimed at people who already know the business. Use the audience it has, even a small one, and two or three channels the team can keep up with. Treat it as a test. By the end you should know which channel sold and what people asked.

## Ask the user first

The renewal price, refund policy and launch date are the user's decisions. If one isn't settled, mark it `[CONFIRM: ...]`, list it as a blocker and don't pick one for them.

1. What's launching, who it's for, the price and what the buyer gets. For subscriptions and memberships, ask what members get on day one and what comes later.
2. The launch offer, if any: exactly what it is, when it ends, and what founding buyers pay at renewal.
3. The launch date, and whether it can move.
4. The audience they already have: list size and typical click rate, followers and engagement on each account, communities they've taken part in (and for how long), partners, past customers, booked guest spots.
5. Past launch results, even for a different product.
6. Who's on the team, and how many hours a week each person has for this.
7. What exists already: demo, screenshots, testimonials from beta users or early customers, the product page.
8. The goal in numbers (sales, members, signups) and the date it applies to.

## Procedure

1. Check the offer. In one sentence, say what it is and who it's for. Add what it replaces if that's true and helps. Then give the price, and what happens right after purchase. If any of these is unclear, list it as a blocker for the user, and point to landing-page-copy for the page.
2. Run the expectation math early, at least four weeks out, because partners take time to arrange. Use the rules below. If the likely case is under half the goal, give the user three options, each with what it would take: lower the goal, move the deadline, or add reach through a partner (partner-outreach finds and pitches them).
3. Set how results are counted. Give each channel its own UTM link. Channels that can't carry a link (podcasts, talks, print) get a spoken code. If the launch offer is a discount code, make one code per channel with the same discount. Add a "How did you hear about us?" question at checkout or in the first email.
4. Choose the channels. List every place the buyers already pay attention. Use "how did you hear" answers and the buyer brief from buyer-research if there is one. Score each place for audience fit, the reach the business has there now, effort, and standing (has the business contributed there before?). Pick two or three channels the team runs itself. One-off placements on other people's channels, such as a podcast guest spot or a sponsored newsletter slot, are partner placements. They don't count against that limit.
5. Lay out the timeline with [references/launch-calendar.md](references/launch-calendar.md): warm-up from about four weeks out, a preview the week before, launch day, a follow-up week, a retrospective on day 14, and a final count on the goal date.
6. List every asset with an owner and a due date: page, demo, screenshots, each email, each post, partner kit, FAQ, support replies, tracking links. Drafts are due at least two weeks out, and final versions two days before they're needed.
7. Add up the hours the timeline needs, against the hours the team has. Cut until the hours fit. Drop a channel before you drop the follow-up week or the test purchases.
8. Write the launch emails and posts. Start from one core idea and rewrite it for each channel. Each post should be worth reading even if nobody clicks. For communities, read the rules first. If you can't read them, plan no link posts there until the user checks. Say that you made the product.
9. Plan the launch emails to the existing list. Normally that's an announcement, a follow-up answering the main question, and, only if the offer has an end date, one reminder on the last day or the day before. Leave out buyers once they've bought. If the list has a regular newsletter, put the warm-up inside regular issues and send the launch emails on top.
10. Prepare for questions. Turn the top objections into an FAQ and short ready replies. Decide who answers comments and emails in the first 48 hours, and how fast.
11. Write the launch-day run sheet, hour by hour. Make one real purchase and refund in the week before, and again on the morning of launch, to prove that checkout, delivery and receipts work.
12. Plan what happens after the launch. Sales usually continue after launch week. Set a permanent route to the offer: a line in every newsletter, a page in the site menu, the welcome emails.
13. Run the retrospective on day 14, and record the final count on the goal date, using the template in the reference file. Add new objections and phrases to the buyer brief.

## Decision rules

Expectation math

- Expected sales = people reached × click rate × purchase rate. Use the user's own past numbers where they exist.
- Adjust past rates when the new offer differs. A recurring subscription or a higher price usually converts below a one-off low-price product. Halving the past rate is a cautious start; label it as an assumption.
- Don't multiply one email's result by the number of emails. Reminders mostly reach the same people. If you know unique clickers from a past multi-send campaign, use them. Otherwise, use the single-email figure as the low case and double it as the high case, and label both.
- Example: 2,000 subscribers × 4% clicking × 5% of clickers buying = 4 sales from one email. That's the low case, so the high case is about 8.

Offers and deadlines

- Use a launch price or bonus only if it has an end date the business will enforce, and close it on time. If there's no real deadline, say "launch week" and nothing more.
- For founding prices on subscriptions, state the renewal price on the page before anyone buys.

Channels

- Don't open new social accounts for a launch, because a new account has no reach. A launch platform such as Product Hunt is different: it's a one-day listing, and it works best when the buyers browse there and the maker has people who will show up that day. It counts as one of the team-run channels.
- Community posts: give useful content first, follow each community's self-promotion rules, and disclose that you made it. Backlinko (2026) suggests about three hours a week of participation over time before promoting anything. A launch link from an account with no history usually gets removed.
- Posting times: use the business's own past posts. Without them, pick a sensible time and keep it consistent.

Practical checks

- Plan around seasons. Business audiences go quiet in late December and in the main holiday weeks. A date that matters in the buyer's year, such as a tax deadline or the start of a season, can carry a launch.
- Check sales tax or VAT on receipts, and the consumer cancellation rights for digital products in the buyers' countries, before launch day.
- Judge the launch on purchases, refunds, cancellations and the questions asked, per channel. Impressions and likes don't count.

## Example

A solo developer is launching a $12 Mac menu-bar app that shows several time zones and finds meeting times across them. They have 1,100 newsletter subscribers from a blog about remote work, 3,200 followers on one social network who reply regularly, and two years of answers in a remote-work subreddit. There are 40 beta users.

- Channels: the newsletter, the subreddit (the developer has standing there, and makers may post on Saturdays with a flair), and a launch-platform listing on day 1 with beta testimonials.
- Math: 1,100 × 6% clicking × 8% buying gives about 5 sales from the launch email, and up to 10 across all sends. The goal was 100. The developer chose to add a partner placement, a sponsored slot in a 15,000-reader remote-work newsletter in launch week. They also moved the goal date out by a month.
- Launch offer: $9 for the first 7 days, ending at 11:59 pm Pacific on day 7. The end time is stated on the page.
- A line from the subreddit post: "I've been answering time-zone questions here for two years, and I finally built the tool I kept describing. Mod-approved maker post, happy to answer anything."

## Final check

- [ ] The offer fits in one clear sentence, and any open decisions are flagged for the user.
- [ ] Every deadline is real and written down. Founding renewal prices are stated.
- [ ] The goal has a number, a date and a counting method. Every channel and placement has its own link or code, and a "how did you hear" question is set up.
- [ ] Expectation math shows low and high cases with labeled assumptions. If the likely case was under half the goal, the user has options.
- [ ] There are no more than three channels the team runs itself, and the hours fit.
- [ ] Every asset has an owner, a draft date and a final date.
- [ ] Launch emails leave out buyers, and there's at most one reminder before a real close.
- [ ] Test purchases are scheduled for the week before and for launch morning.
- [ ] There's a plan for after the launch, a day-14 retrospective and a final count on the goal date.
