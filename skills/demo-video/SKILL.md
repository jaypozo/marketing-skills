# Demo video

A demo has done its job when a viewer can do the task themselves afterwards, or has seen plainly that the product does it. Write the script before you think about equipment. A muddled script is what sinks most demos, and a better camera won't fix it.

## Ask the user first

The script depends on the real product, so ask for access or a walkthrough first. If you can't see it, tag every button name and screen label `[CONFIRM: ...]` until the user has checked it.

1. The product, and the task the video should show, step by step as it happens in the real product. Demos are recorded on the real product, never on mockups.
2. Who the viewer is: a prospect who has never used it, or a customer stuck on a feature.
3. Where the video will be used. This sets length and shape (see the placement table below).
4. Equipment: phone or camera, microphone, screen recording software.
5. Who appears on camera and where, and whether everyone, including the owner of any premises, has agreed.
6. The next step for viewers: a trial, a free resource, a product page.
7. Any numbers or claims the user wants in the video, with their source.

## Procedure

1. Write the video's job in one line: "Show [viewer] how to [task] in [time]." If there are two tasks, plan two videos. A one-time setup step the task depends on goes in the Setup part of the script. If the setup takes more than a minute, it gets its own short video.
2. Look at what already exists. On YouTube, search the task in the customer's words, note the top 10 titles and lengths, and read the top comments for complaints about what was missing. For social, search the same words in Reels or TikTok and look at the most-viewed clips. Use the customer's words in the title and script.
3. Teach the method as well as the tool. If the viewer's real question is wider than the product ("how do I price my cakes?"), give the method openly in the video and show the product doing the work.
4. Write the script in two columns, VISUAL and AUDIO, with timings, using the structure in [references/script-template.md](references/script-template.md):
   - 0 to 10 seconds: show the end result, and say who the video is for.
   - Setup: what the viewer needs first.
   - The steps, done live in the product. Narrate what you're doing and why.
   - The result, shown on screen.
   - One next step.
5. Time the script. Spoken narration runs at about 150 words a minute. Then add time for silent screen action: clicks, loading, typing. Cut anything that doesn't move the viewer toward the result.
6. Check the script against the truth rules below.
7. Plan the recording with the setup lists in the template file. Adapt the lists to this shoot, and hand them to the user as a checklist. In a kitchen, shop floor or anywhere noisy, record the narration separately at a desk with the best mic available, and use the location footage as pictures under it. A USB desk mic is good for exactly that.
8. Write the edit notes. Cut pauses and mistakes. Zoom in on interface text that would be hard to read on a phone. Add captions. Keep music well under the voice, or leave it out. Don't rely on color alone to show a state change; add a label or an arrow. Add chapters to anything over three minutes.
9. Plan a review. Before publishing, someone from the target audience who hasn't seen the product watches it on a phone. Can they say what to do? Fix the step where they got lost.
10. Package it. Write three titles in the viewer's words. Sketch a thumbnail that shows the result in four words or fewer. The first two lines of the description say what the video shows and give the link. Add chapter timestamps if there are chapters.
11. Plan three to five vertical cut-downs, 15 to 60 seconds each. Each makes one complete point: a single step, or the before and after. Reframe to 9:16 with the interface zoomed in. Keep captions and key text clear of the bottom fifth and the right edge, where the app's buttons sit. Show motion or the result in the first second.
12. Plan the link. Point viewers to what continues this exact task. A trial suits viewers of the main video. A free resource often suits colder viewers of the clips. Say it once in the narration, at the point where it helps, and put it in the description and on the end screen. On Instagram and TikTok, captions can't carry links, so use the profile link or a story link sticker. Links in YouTube Shorts descriptions aren't clickable either; use a related-video link or the channel's links. Tag every link with UTM parameters for its placement.
13. Set the measures. Check at 7 and 28 days: retention at 30 seconds, average percentage viewed, link clicks and signups from the UTM tags. Compare with the channel's own previous videos.

## Placement

| Placement | Length | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| YouTube | What the task needs, usually 2 to 10 minutes | 16:9, chapters if over 3 minutes, end screen |
| Homepage or product page | 30 to 90 seconds, or the full demo behind a play button | Plays muted at first, so captions are burned in. Choose a poster frame that shows the product. |
| Sales or onboarding email | Same video, linked | A thumbnail image or short GIF with a play button that links to the video |
| Reels, Shorts, TikTok | 15 to 60 seconds | 9:16, captions, one point per clip |
| Help center | Shortest complete version of the task | Chapters, no intro |

## Decision rules

- The opening: show the result in the first 10 seconds, before any logo or greeting. Backlinko's 2025 video guide puts the decisive window at 5 to 10 seconds.
- Length: as long as the task needs. Padding shows up as a drop on the retention graph.
- Fix the sound before the picture. If there's money for one thing, buy a $20 to $50 clip-on mic before upgrading the camera.
- Interface text must be readable on a phone screen, or zoomed in the edit.
- Truth rules:
  - Record the real product. Show only features that are live for this viewer. Label staged scenes and sample data as such.
  - If a slow step is sped up or cut, say so on screen ("sped up 8x, takes about 40 seconds"). Tell viewers the real wait too, so they don't think the product froze.
  - Use only real testimonials, with permission. Get releases from anyone recognizable, permission from the owner of any location, and music you have a license for. Filming at a customer's premises doesn't make them an endorser unless they agree to be named.
  - Numbers have to be true for the exact wording. "Used by 500+ bakeries" needs 500 bakeries using it. Signups aren't customers. If the true number is weaker than the one wanted, use the true number or none.
  - Use made-up data in the demo account, never real customer records.
- Retention: look for points where the graph drops more than about 10% within a few seconds. That's where viewers got lost or bored. Fix that pattern in the next video.
- End screen: one main next action. YouTube shows end screens in the last 5 to 20 seconds and allows up to four elements (as of 2026); two or three is plenty.

## Example

A plant-care app identifies watering problems from a photo. The viewer is a prospect whose fiddle-leaf fig is dropping leaves. The video will live on YouTube and on the app's landing page.

Job: "Show a new plant owner how to find out why leaves are dropping, in under three minutes."

| Time | VISUAL | AUDIO |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00 | Phone screen: result card reads "Overwatering, roots likely sitting wet" over a photo of the yellowing fig | "My fig was dropping leaves. This is what the app told me from one photo." |
| 0:05 | Wide shot of the plant by a window, fallen leaves on the floor | "Here's how to check yours in under three minutes." |
| 0:10 | Over-the-shoulder: open the app, tap Diagnose | "Open the app and tap Diagnose. Stand near the window so the light's good." |

Cut-down 1 takes the 0:00 to 0:10 reveal plus the "check the soil" step, reframed to vertical, 22 seconds, with the caption "Leaves dropping? Check this first." It links through the profile to the app's free guide to fixing an overwatered plant.

## Final check

- [ ] The video's job is stated in a line.
- [ ] The result appears in the first 10 seconds.
- [ ] Everything shown is the real product, live for this viewer, at real speed or labeled as sped up.
- [ ] Every number and claim is true for its exact wording.
- [ ] No real customer data. Releases, location permission and music licenses are covered.
- [ ] Interface text is readable on a phone, captions are present, and states don't rely on color alone.
- [ ] Length and shape fit each placement, and homepage video has burned-in captions.
- [ ] Each link goes to a page that continues the task and carries UTM tags. Social clips use the profile or story link.
- [ ] Each cut-down makes sense on its own.
- [ ] Unchecked interface labels and facts carry CONFIRM tags, and the list goes to the user.
