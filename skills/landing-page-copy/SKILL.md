# Landing page copy

The page is for one kind of visitor making one decision. The material comes from the business: what customers say, what the product or service does, and what the business can prove. Put it in the order the buyer needs, and keep out anything the business can't back up.

## Ask the user first

Item 1 can't be guessed, so get the offer first. Write around the rest and mark the gaps (see "Markers").

1. The offer, exactly: what the buyer gets, the price, and how and when it's delivered. For services, also ask for booking, cancellation and no-show terms. For products, ask for refund or guarantee terms. Get the wording from the actual policy.
2. Where visitors arrive from: a search ad (get the ad text), a social ad (get the text and the image or video), an email, a search query, a partner. Ask what those visitors already know.
3. The one action the page is for: buy, book, start a trial, request a quote, call.
4. Evidence they can show: testimonials (and how they may be used), review ratings and counts with the platform, customer numbers, results with dates, certifications, press, a demo.
5. What customers say: the objections section of a buyer brief if one exists, or raw reviews, support questions and sales call notes.
6. The current page or draft, and its conversion rate if they know it, as the baseline.
7. A sample of writing in the brand's voice.
8. Constraints: regulated claims (health, finance, legal), words they never use, required disclaimers.

## Markers

Use these in the draft so the user can see every gap at a glance:

- `[ASSUMPTION: ...]` a guess about the buyer, where no evidence was given.
- `[CONFIRM: ...]` a fact about the offer or the operation that's missing (a size range, a service area, what happens on late cancellation).
- `[NEEDS PROOF: ...]` a claim the user wants that the evidence doesn't support yet, with the evidence that would allow it. This marker goes in the claims table only. In the copy, use the strongest line the evidence does support.

## Procedure

1. Decide which job this is.
   - A blank page, or a page with no clear argument (a generic homepage counts), means you build the argument.
   - A draft with a clear voice means you keep the voice and fix the structure and the weak lines.
   - A request to "fix the headline" means you change only the headline and return the rest untouched.
2. Write a five-line message brief: who arrives, what they arrived from, the promise, the strongest proof, the top objection. Put it at the top of the output, and carry on.
3. Check message match. The headline has to answer what brought the visitor, in close to the same words. If the ad says "restaurant scheduling app", the page can't open with "Run a happier team". Social ads also need visual continuity: the first screen should look like the ad that was clicked. Visitors from social ads didn't search for anything. Expect them to know less, and give the problem section more room.
4. Set the length. Write as many words as it takes to answer the objections in the evidence, and stop there. Cold traffic, an unfamiliar category, a higher price or a buyer who has to justify the purchase all add objections. Warm traffic, a familiar category and a low price remove them.
5. Write five headlines from different angles: the outcome, the problem, a specific claim with a number, a call-out to the audience, and a line in the customer's own words. Check each one. Can a stranger tell what this is and who it's for in five seconds? Is there something concrete in it? Could a competitor use it unchanged? If message match and the competitor test pull in different directions, message match wins in the headline, and the subhead carries what makes this business different. Keep the best headline and two alternates. The scoring stays in your notes.
6. Draft the sections in the order in [references/page-sections.md](references/page-sections.md), which also covers the extra sections a local service or booking page needs. Cut sections the decision doesn't need.
7. For each feature, write what it means for the buyer, the way they'd say it. "Shift swaps need manager approval" becomes "Staff swap shifts themselves, and you approve with one tap."
8. Put each piece of proof next to the claim it supports. A testimonial about speed goes beside the speed claim, not in a block at the bottom. Use a testimonial as a headline, or shorten it, only if the customer's permission covers that.
9. Answer the top three objections in plain words. Each one gets one place: the body if it's a reason not to buy, the FAQ if it's a practical question. If the evidence has no answer to an objection, don't make one up. Put it in the open questions for the user.
10. Write the call to action as a verb plus what they get ("Book your groom", "Start the 14-day trial"). The line under the button removes the biggest remaining worry. If that worry is what happens next, say what happens next. On long pages, repeat the same action after each major section.
11. Write the first phone screen deliberately. Assume most ad traffic is on phones unless the analytics say otherwise. The headline, subhead, button and one line of proof should all fit there.
12. Run the claims check (below). Then reread the page as a stranger would, and cut every adjective that carries no information.

## Decision rules

Writing

- Keep the headline under about 12 words and let the subhead do the explaining.
- Keep paragraphs to one to three lines.
- Use the customer's words for the problem, even when they're less polished than the company's.
- One primary action per page. On pages for paid traffic, remove the main site menu and links that lead away. For local services, keep a tap-to-call number: it leads to the same booking, so it doesn't count as a second action.

Claims

- Every factual claim needs a source the business can show: a policy, data, a review, a certificate.
- Invent nothing: no testimonials, numbers, logos, review counts, awards, scarcity or deadlines. Real limits are fine when stated plainly and kept current ("Usually booked two to three weeks ahead").
- Claims marked NEEDS PROOF stay out of the final copy. Put them in the claims table, and use the strongest line the evidence does support instead.
- Some claims can never be proven, such as "best in town" or "stress-free for every dog". Mark them Can't support in the table, and rewrite them as something specific and true.

Testing

- Test big differences first (a different promise or page structure) before small ones. For sample sizes and test length, use conversion-diagnosis.
- Benchmarks are context only. A 2026 roundup of landing page data published by Backlinko reported a 6.6% median conversion rate across nine industries, from 3.8% for SaaS to 12.3% for events and entertainment. The page's own past rate is the baseline that counts.

## Output

Deliver these parts, in order:

1. The message brief (five lines).
2. The page copy in section order. Each block gets a label a designer can use: HERO HEADLINE, SUBHEAD, CTA BUTTON, UNDER BUTTON, SECTION 2 HEADING, and so on.
3. Headline alternates: two lines, each with the angle it tests.
4. A claims table:

| Claim | Source | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Build next week's schedule in about ten minutes" | Median time from product analytics, Q2 | Supported |
| "Trusted by 2,000 restaurants" | None given | NEEDS PROOF: active account count |
| "The best scheduling app" | None possible | Can't support. Used "Rated 4.8 on the App Store (1,240 reviews)" instead. |

5. Open questions, and every CONFIRM item, as a list for the user.

## Example

Employee scheduling software for restaurants, $4 per staff member per month. Traffic comes from a search ad for "restaurant staff scheduling app". The top objection in the evidence is that older staff won't use another app.

```text
HERO HEADLINE
Restaurant staff scheduling your team can use from a text message

SUBHEAD
Build next week's schedule in about ten minutes. Staff get it on their phone, swap shifts with each other, and you approve with one tap.

CTA BUTTON
Start the 14-day trial
UNDER BUTTON
No card needed. Import your staff list from a spreadsheet.

SECTION 2 HEADING
"Half my servers are over fifty. They figured it out on the first day."
BODY
Staff log in with a text message link, so there's no password to forget. The schedule looks like the paper one on the office wall, one row per day...
```

The headline repeats the ad's words and answers the main objection with a fact about how staff log in. The testimonial sits next to the claim it supports. "Most staff log in within 24 hours" was wanted, but there was no activation data, so it went into the claims table as NEEDS PROOF and not onto the page.

## Final check

- [ ] The headline matches the ad, email or query that sends traffic, and the first screen matches a social ad's look.
- [ ] A stranger could say what's sold and who it's for after reading only the first phone screen.
- [ ] There's one primary action. Its button says what the visitor gets, and the line under it removes the biggest worry.
- [ ] The top three objections are answered, each in one place, or listed as open questions.
- [ ] Price, delivery, booking and refund or cancellation terms match the user's policy.
- [ ] Every claim is in the claims table. No NEEDS PROOF or Can't support claim is in the copy.
- [ ] No invented testimonials, numbers, logos, urgency or scarcity.
- [ ] The user has a list of every ASSUMPTION and CONFIRM marker.
- [ ] No sentence could sit unchanged on a competitor's page, apart from the headline words that match the ad.
